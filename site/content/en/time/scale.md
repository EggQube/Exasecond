The universe is about 435,000,000,000,000,000 seconds old. The shortest span of time anyone has measured is about 0.000000000000000000247 seconds. Between those two numbers lie 36 powers of ten, and physics reaches further in both directions. Human intuition covers perhaps eight of them, from a blink to a long life. Everything else has to be reached with instruments and arithmetic.

This chapter is a map of that range. The rest of this work explains how we know what is on it.

## Counting in powers of ten

The International System of Units (SI) names powers of a thousand with prefixes. Most are familiar from bytes and metres. Applied to seconds, the large ones are rarely used, because people switch to minutes, days and years instead. This site keeps them, because they make the scale honest.

| Prefix | Seconds | Roughly | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| kilosecond (ks) | 10^3 | 17 minutes | a short lecture |
| megasecond (Ms) | 10^6 | 11.6 days | a long holiday |
| gigasecond (Gs) | 10^9 | 31.7 years | a generation |
| terasecond (Ts) | 10^12 | 31,700 years | longer than every city, farm and written word |
| petasecond (Ps) | 10^15 | 31.7 million years | since the Antarctic ice sheet began to form, roughly |
| exasecond (Es) | 10^18 | 31.7 billion years | more than twice the age of the universe |

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures added four new prefixes: ronna (10^27) and quetta (10^30) for the very large, ronto (10^-27) and quecto (10^-30) for the very small. Nothing in nature is yet known to last a quettasecond, and nothing has been measured as briefly as a rontosecond.

## The very short

**The Planck time, 5.39 × 10^-44 s.** This is the time light takes to cross one Planck length. It is built from three constants of nature: the speed of light, the gravitational constant and the reduced Planck constant. It is not a measured "pixel" of time. It marks the scale below which our two best theories, general relativity and quantum mechanics, would both have to be used at once, and we do not have a tested theory that does that. Below it, nobody knows whether "duration" still means anything. See [What is time? Open questions](/time/nature).

**Zeptoseconds (10^-21 s).** In 2020 a team at Goethe University Frankfurt measured how long a photon takes to cross a hydrogen molecule: about 247 zeptoseconds. It is the shortest time interval measured directly so far.

**Attoseconds (10^-18 s).** Electrons move around atoms on this timescale. Pulses of light a few dozen attoseconds long let physicists film that motion. The technique earned the 2023 Nobel Prize in Physics for Pierre Agostini, Ferenc Krausz and Anne L'Huillier.

**Femtoseconds (10^-15 s).** Chemical bonds vibrate and break in tens to hundreds of femtoseconds. Ahmed Zewail won the 1999 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for watching reactions happen at this speed.

**Picoseconds and nanoseconds (10^-12 and 10^-9 s).** Light travels about 30 centimetres in a nanosecond. A processor running at 3 GHz completes one clock cycle every third of a nanosecond. One cycle of the caesium-133 radiation that defines the second lasts about 0.109 nanoseconds.

## The human range

A heartbeat takes about a second. That is not a coincidence. The second descends from ancient divisions of the day into 24 hours of 60 minutes of 60 seconds, units sized to human life (see [What is a second?](/time/second)).

A day is 86,400 seconds. A year is about 31.6 million seconds (31,556,952 for the average Gregorian year). An 80-year life is about 2.5 gigaseconds. Writing is about 5,200 years old, or 0.16 terasecond. Our species, Homo sapiens, has existed for about 300,000 years, roughly 9.5 teraseconds.

## The very long

**Geological time (petaseconds).** The non-avian dinosaurs died out 66 million years ago, about 2.1 petaseconds. Complex animal life appeared in the Cambrian, about 539 million years ago (17 petaseconds).

**Planetary and cosmic time (fractions of an exasecond).** The Earth is 4.54 billion years old, 0.143 exasecond. The universe is about 13.8 billion years old, 0.435 exasecond. One full exasecond, 31.7 billion years, lies in the future. Some cosmological models published in 2025 suggest the universe might not last that long; most do not. See [The far future](/time/future).

**Beyond the exasecond.** Some atomic nuclei decay so slowly that their half-lives are trillions of times the age of the universe. Xenon-124 has a measured half-life of about 1.1 × 10^22 years (XENON collaboration, 2019 and 2022), and tellurium-128 about 2 × 10^24 years. Experiments looking for proton decay have not seen one; the proton's lifetime, if finite, is longer than about 10^34 years. These numbers are measured by watching very large amounts of material: a half-life of 10^22 years means that in a tonne of natural xenon, only a few hundred atoms of xenon-124 decay in a year.

## Why the scale matters

On a logarithmic scale the universe's early history and our own history are both crowded together. The universe's first second spans more powers of ten (from 10^-43 s to 1 s) than everything since (from 1 s to about 10^17.6 s). And all of recorded history fits in the last 0.00004 percent of the universe's age.

Exasecond time, the clock on this site, counts on the linear scale: one second after another, from an agreed beginning. The [specification](/spec.md) explains the convention.
