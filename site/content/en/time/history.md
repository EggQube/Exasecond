This chapter tells the history of the universe on the clock this site keeps: seconds since the beginning. The times come from the standard model of cosmology, called ΛCDM (Lambda for dark energy, CDM for cold dark matter), fitted to measurements of the cosmic microwave background and of the large-scale structure of the universe. For Earth's history they come from radiometric dating of rocks and meteorites.

How sure we are depends on the era. From about one second onward, the story rests on well-tested physics and direct observations. Before that, it rests on extrapolation of particle physics tested in accelerators. Before about 10^-11 seconds, it is increasingly theory. Before 10^-43 seconds, nobody knows (see [The beginning](/time/beginning)).

## The first second

**The Planck epoch (before ~10^-43 s).** At these times the energy densities were so high that quantum effects of gravity must have mattered. No tested theory describes them. **Evidence: none yet.**

**Inflation (somewhere around 10^-36 to 10^-32 s, if it happened).** A leading hypothesis says that space expanded exponentially, by a factor of at least 10^26, in a tiny fraction of a second. It explains why the universe is so uniform and so flat, and it predicts the pattern of slight density ripples seen in the cosmic microwave background. The exact timing and mechanism are unknown. **Evidence: supported, not confirmed.** A key prediction, primordial gravitational waves, has not been detected yet.

**Electroweak epoch (~10^-12 s).** As the universe cooled below about 10^15 kelvin, the electromagnetic and weak forces separated, and particles acquired mass through the Higgs field. The Higgs boson, found at CERN in 2012, confirmed the mechanism. **Evidence: established physics; the cosmological details are extrapolated.**

**Quark epoch to hadron epoch (~10^-5 s).** Quarks and gluons, which had formed a hot soup (quark-gluon plasma, recreated in particle colliders since 2000), bound together into protons and neutrons. A tiny excess of matter over antimatter, about one extra particle per billion pairs, survived the annihilation that followed. Why that excess exists is an open question.

**Neutrino decoupling (~1 s).** Neutrinos stopped interacting with other matter and have streamed freely ever since. This cosmic neutrino background has not been detected directly, but its effects on the cosmic microwave background and on the abundance of light elements have been measured.

## Minutes to millennia

**Big Bang nucleosynthesis (~10 s to 20 minutes).** The universe was a nuclear reactor. Protons and neutrons fused into deuterium, helium-4, some helium-3 and traces of lithium-7. The theory predicts that about 25 percent of normal matter by mass ended up as helium, and that is what astronomers measure in the oldest gas clouds. **Evidence: established.** (Lithium is the exception: observed old stars hold about three times less of it than predicted, the "lithium problem".)

**The plasma era (20 minutes to 380,000 years).** The universe was a hot glowing plasma of nuclei, electrons and light, opaque like the inside of the Sun. Sound waves rippled through it. Matter came to dominate over radiation around 47,000 years (1.5 × 10^12 s).

**Recombination (about 380,000 years, 1.2 × 10^13 s).** Below about 3,000 kelvin, electrons joined nuclei to form neutral atoms, and the universe became transparent. The light released at that moment still fills space, stretched by the expansion into microwaves at 2.7 kelvin: the **cosmic microwave background** (CMB), discovered by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson in 1965. Its tiny temperature variations, one part in 100,000, mapped by the COBE, WMAP and Planck satellites, are the seeds of all later structure. **Evidence: established.**

## The dark ages and the first light

**Dark ages (380,000 years to ~100 million years).** No stars yet. Gravity slowly pulled gas toward the denser regions of dark matter.

**First stars (perhaps 100 to 200 million years, ~3 to 6 × 10^15 s).** The first stars, called Population III, were probably massive and short-lived, made of pure hydrogen and helium. None has been observed directly. **Evidence: predicted; searched for with the James Webb Space Telescope.**

**First galaxies.** The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in December 2021, has found galaxies earlier and brighter than many models expected. The most distant confirmed so far, MoM-z14, at redshift 14.44, is seen as it was about 280 million years after the beginning (8.8 × 10^15 s).

**Reionization (to ~1 billion years, 3.2 × 10^16 s).** Ultraviolet light from the first stars and galaxies stripped electrons from the hydrogen between galaxies again. By about a billion years, the process was complete.

## Galaxies, the Sun and the Earth

Galaxies merged and grew. Star formation across the universe peaked around 3.5 billion years after the beginning, a period astronomers call "cosmic noon", and has declined since. About 7.7 billion years after the beginning, some 6 billion years ago, the expansion of the universe stopped slowing down and began to accelerate, driven by dark energy (discovered in 1998, Nobel Prize 2011).

"Time ago" is counted back from the year 2000. The universe seconds in the table are calculated with this site's convention (the universe was exactly 13,787,000,000 Julian years old at the start of 2000). The real ages carry uncertainties: about ±20 million years for the age of the universe, a few million years for the Earth, and much more for the early fossils, so the trailing digits are arithmetic, not knowledge.

## Today

The universe is now about 0.435 exaseconds old. It is about 68 percent dark energy, 27 percent dark matter and 5 percent ordinary matter, by energy density. Neither dark energy nor dark matter has been identified. The expansion continues to accelerate. How it will end is the subject of [The far future](/time/future).
