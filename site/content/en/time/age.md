title: How we know the age of the universe
description: 13.8 billion years, measured three independent ways, and the "Hubble tension": a real disagreement about how fast the universe is expanding.
---
The universe is about 13.8 billion years old. Planck's 2018 analysis gives 13.787 ± 0.020 billion years, an uncertainty of about 0.15 percent. That number is what this site's clock is anchored to. This chapter explains where it comes from, why independent methods agree, and where they do not.

## A short history of a big number

In 1927 Georges Lemaître showed that Einstein's equations allow an expanding universe and linked it to the redshifts of galaxies. In 1929 Edwin Hubble published the relation between distance and recession speed, now called the Hubble-Lemaître law: the farther a galaxy, the faster it recedes.

If galaxies are flying apart, run the film backwards and they were once together. The time since then is roughly 1 divided by the expansion rate, the **Hubble constant**, H₀. Hubble's own value, about 500 km/s per megaparsec, gave an age of only about 2 billion years, younger than the Earth as dated by radioactivity. The contradiction was resolved in the 1950s when Walter Baade and Allan Sandage found that Hubble's distance scale was badly off. The age grew to 10 to 20 billion years, and over the following decades the range narrowed.

## Method 1: the cosmic microwave background

The most precise age comes from the cosmic microwave background (CMB), the light released 380,000 years after the beginning (see [A history of the universe](/time/history)). Its temperature varies across the sky by about one part in 100,000. The pattern of those variations, especially the characteristic size of hot and cold spots, depends on the amount of ordinary matter, dark matter and dark energy, and on the geometry of space.

Fitting the standard cosmological model (ΛCDM) to the Planck satellite's maps gives the composition of the universe and, from it, the time since the beginning: **13.787 ± 0.020 billion years**. Ground-based telescopes, the Atacama Cosmology Telescope (final results, 2025) and the South Pole Telescope, find consistent values.

This is a model-dependent number: it assumes ΛCDM is correct. Within that model, it is very precise. **Evidence: established**, with the caveat that the model itself is under test (see below).

## Method 2: the oldest stars

The universe cannot be younger than the oldest things in it. Stars give an independent lower limit, using completely different physics: nuclear fusion and stellar structure instead of cosmology.

- **Globular clusters** are dense balls of hundreds of thousands of stars that formed together. The brightness at which their stars leave the main sequence acts as a clock. A 2020 analysis of 38 clusters by David Valcin and colleagues found an age for the universe of 13.5 billion years, with an uncertainty of about half a billion years.
- **Radioactive dating of stars.** Some very old stars contain measurable uranium and thorium. Their decay works like carbon dating on a cosmic scale. The star HE 1523-0901 was dated this way to about 13.2 billion years (Frebel and colleagues, 2007).
- **White dwarf cooling.** White dwarfs are the cooling cores of dead stars. The faintest, coolest ones in a population tell how long it has been cooling: the oldest parts of our galaxy come out at roughly 11 to 13 billion years.

All of these are younger than 13.8 billion years, and none are clearly older. **Evidence: consistent with Method 1.**

## Method 3: the expansion rate today

Measure H₀ directly, from nearby galaxies, and you get a rough age without the CMB. That is where the trouble begins.

The distance ladder works in steps. Parallax gives distances to nearby stars. Those calibrate pulsating stars called **Cepheids** (or old red giants at the "tip of the red giant branch"), visible in nearby galaxies. Those, in turn, calibrate **type Ia supernovae**, exploding white dwarfs bright enough to see across billions of light years. Supernovae in galaxies moving with the expansion then give H₀.

| Method | H₀ (km/s per megaparsec) |
|---|---|
| CMB, Planck 2018 (assuming ΛCDM) | 67.4 ± 0.5 |
| CMB, ACT + Planck + DESI (2025) | 68.4 ± 0.3 |
| Cepheids and supernovae, SH0ES (Riess et al. 2022) | 73.0 ± 1.0 |
| Red giant stars and supernovae, CCHP (Freedman et al. 2024) | 70.4 ± 1.9 |
| Gravitational waves from merging neutron stars (2017) | 70, with an uncertainty of about ±10 |

## The Hubble tension

The early-universe value (about 67 to 68) and the SH0ES local value (about 73) differ by about five times their combined uncertainty, far more than chance would explain. This is the **Hubble tension**. Two kinds of explanation are being pursued:

1. **A measurement problem.** Some step of the distance ladder may be miscalibrated. The James Webb Space Telescope has re-observed Cepheids with sharper resolution; the SH0ES team finds its value confirmed, while the Chicago-Carnegie team, using other kinds of stars, gets values closer to 70. The disagreement between those two teams has not been settled.
2. **New physics.** ΛCDM might be incomplete: for example an extra form of energy in the early universe ("early dark energy"), or dark energy that changes over time. The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) reported in 2024 and 2025 hints that dark energy may be weakening, at a significance of roughly 3 to 4 standard deviations depending on the data combined. That is interesting but not yet decisive. No proposed new physics resolves the tension cleanly.

Why it matters for age: within ΛCDM, a faster expansion means a younger universe. With H₀ = 73 and nothing else changed, the age would come out around 12.6 to 13 billion years, starting to conflict with the oldest stars. **Status: open.** The age of 13.8 billion years is well established within the standard model; how much it could change if the model changes is part of the open question, and is likely to be a few percent at most.

## Light-years and the size of the observable universe

If the universe is 13.8 billion years old, how can we see galaxies that are now more than 30 billion light years away? Because space expanded while their light was travelling. "Light travel time" (how long the light took), "distance when the light was emitted" and "distance now" are three different numbers. The radius of the observable universe, measured as distance now, is about 46 billion light years.

## Why the site uses 13.787 billion years

A clock needs an exact number, not a range. Exasecond time therefore fixes, by convention, that the universe was exactly 13,787,000,000 Julian years old at the start of the year 2000, using the Planck 2018 central value. If future measurements shift the best estimate, the convention stays: like the metre or the calendar, it is an agreement, not a claim. See the [specification](/spec.md).
