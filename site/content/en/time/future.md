---
title: The far future
description: What happens to the Sun, the galaxy and the universe over the next exaseconds and beyond, and the open question that decides it all: what is dark energy?
---
Predicting the far future is easier than it sounds for some things and impossible for others. The life of the Sun follows from well-tested stellar physics. The fate of the universe as a whole depends on dark energy, which nobody understands. This chapter goes from the near and confident to the far and uncertain.

## The Sun and the Earth

**Supported by well-tested stellar models.**

- **In about 1 billion years** the Sun, which brightens by roughly 10 percent per billion years, will be bright enough to start evaporating the Earth's oceans. Complex life on the surface is likely to end around then.
- **In about 5 billion years** the Sun runs out of hydrogen in its core and swells into a red giant, reaching its largest about 7.5 billion years from now. Mercury and Venus will be swallowed; whether the Earth is too is uncertain, because the Sun will lose mass and the planets' orbits will widen.
- Then the Sun sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula, and its core remains as a **white dwarf**, about the size of the Earth, slowly cooling for trillions of years.

## The galaxy

The Andromeda galaxy is approaching the Milky Way at about 110 kilometres per second. For years a collision about 4.5 billion years from now was considered almost certain. A 2025 study by Till Sawala and colleagues, using new data from the Gaia and Hubble telescopes and including the influence of other nearby galaxies, found only about a 50 percent chance of a merger within the next 10 billion years. **Status: uncertain.** Even if they collide, stars are so far apart that almost none would hit each other.

## The next exasecond

This site counts time in exaseconds. The universe is now about 0.435 Es old. The time remaining until it is 1 Es old, about 17.9 billion years, is longer than its whole history so far. If dark energy behaves as a cosmological constant (the simplest assumption, and so far consistent with most data), nothing dramatic happens to the universe as a whole in that time. Galaxies beyond our own local group move ever faster away.

## If dark energy is constant: the long fade

**Supported as the default projection.** Fred Adams and Gregory Laughlin described the future of a forever-expanding universe in a 1997 paper, dividing it into eras by powers of ten in years:

| Years from now | What happens |
|---|---|
| ~10^11 | All galaxies outside our merged Local Group have receded beyond view. Future astronomers could not see the expansion of the universe or the cosmic microwave background, which will have faded and stretched beyond detection. |
| ~10^12 to 10^14 | Galaxies run out of gas. The last red dwarf stars, the longest-lived stars, burn out. The **stelliferous era**, the age of stars, ends. |
| ~10^15 to 10^20 | The **degenerate era**: white dwarfs, neutron stars and black holes. Close encounters fling most stars out of galaxies; the rest spiral into central black holes. |
| > 10^34, if protons decay | Matter itself dissolves, if protons are unstable as many grand unified theories predict. None has been seen to decay so far. |
| ~10^67 to 10^100 | The **black hole era**. Black holes slowly evaporate by Hawking radiation: one of the Sun's mass in about 10^67 years, the largest in about 10^100. |
| afterwards | The **dark era**: a thin, cooling gas of photons, neutrinos and electrons, ever more spread out. Nothing more happens that could store or process information: the "heat death". |

These numbers are for a universe whose dark energy stays exactly constant, and they extrapolate known physics far beyond where it has been tested.

## If dark energy grows: the Big Rip

**Speculative, disfavoured by current data.** If dark energy became denser as space expands (a "phantom" equation of state, w < −1), the acceleration would grow without limit and eventually tear apart galaxies, solar systems, planets and finally atoms, at a finite time in the future. Robert Caldwell, Marc Kamionkowski and Nevin Weinberg worked out this scenario in 2003. Current measurements show no sign of it; if anything, the latest hints point the other way.

## If dark energy weakens: a Big Crunch?

**Speculative, actively studied.** In 2024 and 2025 the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), whose second data release maps more than 14 million galaxies and quasars, reported hints that dark energy may have been weakening over the last few billion years. The significance is about 3 to 4 standard deviations depending on which other data are combined, not yet enough to be called a discovery. Results from the Euclid satellite and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory should help settle it.

If dark energy keeps weakening and eventually turns negative, the expansion could stop and reverse into a collapse: a **Big Crunch**. Hoang Nhan Luu, Yu-Cheng Qiu and Henry Tye fitted one such model to the new data in 2025 and found a total lifespan of about 33 billion years, meaning the universe would end roughly 20 billion years from now. That would be about 1.05 exaseconds in total. The authors note that the result depends strongly on their model's assumptions and that the data allow a wide range of outcomes. **Status: one model among several; not a prediction the field agrees on.**

## Vacuum decay

**Speculative.** The masses of the Higgs boson (125 GeV) and the top quark, as measured at CERN, suggest that the vacuum of our universe may be **metastable**: not the lowest possible energy state. A bubble of lower-energy vacuum could in principle form somewhere and expand at nearly the speed of light, changing the laws of physics inside it. Calculations using the Standard Model give an expected lifetime vastly longer than 10^100 years, and the conclusion could change entirely with new physics beyond the Standard Model. It is not a practical concern.

## What would settle it

The fate of the universe depends almost entirely on the nature of dark energy. The next decade of surveys (DESI, Euclid, Rubin, the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, and the Chinese Space Station Telescope, CSST) will measure how the expansion has changed over time with much greater precision. If dark energy turns out to be exactly constant, the long fade is the answer. If not, the future is open again.
