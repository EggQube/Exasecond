---
title: Daylight saving time
description: Why about seventy countries move their clocks twice a year, who started it, who stopped, why Europe and the USA change on different days, and what the evidence says about energy, sleep and health.
---
Twice a year, in about seventy countries, clocks jump: forward an hour in spring, back an hour in autumn. The Sun does not change; the numbers on the clock do. The idea is to shift the hours people are awake toward the light of long summer evenings. Whether that is worth the jump is one of the longest-running arguments about time.

**Status, October 2026:** the European Union still changes its clocks (back on Sunday 25 October 2026); a plan to stop has been stuck since 2019. The United States changes back on Sunday 1 November 2026; a bill for permanent summer time passed the House of Representatives in July 2026 and awaits the Senate.

## A joke, an entomologist and a builder

Benjamin Franklin is often credited with the idea, but his 1784 letter to the *Journal de Paris* was satire: he suggested that Parisians could save candles by getting up at dawn, enforced by cannon fire and taxes on shutters. He did not propose changing clocks.

The real proposals came later. In 1895 George Vernon Hudson, an entomologist in New Zealand who wanted more daylight after work to collect insects, proposed a two-hour summer shift. In 1907 William Willett, a London builder, published *The Waste of Daylight*, arguing for moving clocks forward in four steps of 20 minutes each spring. A bill went before the British Parliament in 1908 and failed. Willett died in 1915, a year before his idea became law.

## War and coal

The first country to adopt it was Germany, together with Austria-Hungary, on 30 April 1916, in the middle of the First World War, to save coal for the war effort. Britain followed three weeks later, and many other countries soon after. The United States introduced it in 1918, repealed it the next year, and brought it back nationally during the Second World War as "War Time". (A few places had tried it earlier on their own: the town of Port Arthur, Ontario, now Thunder Bay, moved its clocks in the summer of 1908.)

After each war, many countries dropped it; with the oil crises of the 1970s, many brought it back. The United States even tried year-round daylight saving time in 1974, during the energy crisis. It was popular at first and quickly unpopular when children went to school in the dark; it was ended early.

## Who changes, and when

About 70 of the world's countries change their clocks, roughly a third. Most are in Europe and North America, plus parts of the Middle East, Oceania and South America. Near the equator day length hardly varies, so most tropical countries never adopted it. Most of Asia and Africa do not use it; China tried it from 1986 to 1991, and Japan only under American occupation, from 1948 to 1951.

| Region | Clocks go forward | Clocks go back |
|---|---|---|
| European Union and the UK | last Sunday in March, 01:00 UTC | last Sunday in October, 01:00 UTC |
| United States and Canada | second Sunday in March, 2:00 local | first Sunday in November, 2:00 local |
| South-eastern Australia | first Sunday in October | first Sunday in April |
| New Zealand | last Sunday in September | first Sunday in April |

In the southern hemisphere the seasons are reversed, so the clocks move the opposite way: when Europe falls back in October, Sydney has just sprung forward.

All EU countries change at the same instant, 01:00 UTC, so their relative times never shift. North America changes at 2:00 in the morning local time, zone by zone.

### Out of step

The United States moved its dates in 2007 (Energy Policy Act of 2005) and Europe did not. So every spring there are two or three weeks, and every autumn one week, when the gap between them is an hour smaller than usual. Between 25 October and 1 November 2026, New York is four hours behind London instead of five. Meetings scheduled across the Atlantic go wrong every year in those weeks.

In the United States, Arizona (except the Navajo Nation) and Hawaii do not change their clocks.

## Europe: the vote that stalled

In 2018 the European Commission held a public consultation. It received about 4.6 million responses, the most ever, and 84 percent wanted the changes to end (although most responses came from Germany). The Commission proposed abolishing seasonal clock changes, and in March 2019 the European Parliament voted in favour, with 2021 as the target year. Each country would choose whether to stay on summer or winter time permanently.

The Council of the EU, where the member states sit, has never agreed a position. Countries worry about ending up with a patchwork: Paris and Berlin on different times, for example, if one chose summer time and the other winter time. Spain revived the proposal in October 2025. As of October 2026 the directive from 2000 that sets the changes is still in force, and dates have been published up to 2031.

## The United States: permanent summer?

The **Sunshine Protection Act** would put the United States on daylight saving time all year. It passed the Senate without objection in 2022 but was never taken up by the House, and expired. A new version passed the House of Representatives on 14 July 2026, by 308 votes to 117. As of October 2026 it awaits the Senate. Sleep scientists and some senators oppose permanent summer time and argue for permanent standard time instead (see below).

## What the evidence says

### Energy
**Evidence: small and mixed.** The original reason was to save fuel. Modern studies find little effect: a 2008 US Department of Energy study of the 2007 extension found electricity savings of about 0.5 percent per day during the extra weeks, about 0.03 percent of the year's total. A study of Indiana, which adopted daylight saving time statewide in 2006, found residential electricity use went **up** by about 1 percent, as evening cooling outweighed savings on lighting.

### Sleep and health
**Evidence: consistent for the spring change, mostly from observational studies.** The spring change costs an hour of sleep, and the body clock takes days to adjust.
- **Heart attacks:** a 2019 meta-analysis found about a 5 percent higher risk of heart attack in the week after the spring change.
- **Traffic:** a 2020 study of US data found about 6 percent more fatal car crashes in the week after the spring change.
- **Long term:** sleep scientists argue that permanent summer time would leave the body clock out of step with the Sun all winter, with dark mornings. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends permanent **standard** time, not permanent summer time. The long-term effects of year-round daylight saving time are less studied than the effects of the change itself.

### Daylight itself
Clock changes do not create daylight. On the shortest day in Oslo the Sun is up for about six hours whatever the clock says; daylight saving time only decides whether people meet that light in the morning or the evening.

## Why it matters for machines

The rules are national laws that change. Software must look them up in the time zone database (see [Time zones](/time/zones) and [World time](/time/timescales)), and it must handle the hour that happens twice in autumn (in New York, 01:30 occurs twice that night; in Oslo, 02:30) and the hour that never happens in spring. When a country changes its rules at short notice, computers, phones and scheduled meetings are wrong until the database is updated.
