---
title: The arrow of time
description: The basic laws of physics barely care which way time runs. Eggs break and never unbreak because of entropy, and because the universe began in a very special state.
---
Film a planet orbiting a star and run the film backwards: the reversed orbit obeys Newton's laws just as well. Film an egg falling off a table and run it backwards: the shell leaps up and reassembles itself, which never happens. Most of the fundamental laws of physics work the same in both directions of time. Everyday experience is completely one-directional. Explaining that difference is the problem of the **arrow of time**.

## Laws that run both ways

Newton's mechanics, Maxwell's electromagnetism, Einstein's relativity and the Schrödinger equation of quantum mechanics are all, to a very good approximation, **time-reversal symmetric**: for every process they allow, the reversed process is also allowed.

There is one known exception. The weak nuclear force violates time-reversal symmetry slightly. Physicists inferred this in 1964 from the decays of neutral kaons, which also broke a related symmetry called CP; the CPLEAR experiment at CERN observed the time asymmetry directly in 1998, and BaBar at SLAC observed it in B mesons in 2012. Together with charge conjugation (C) and parity (P), the combined CPT symmetry is believed to be exact. The effect is real but tiny and occurs in rare particle decays. It is not believed to be the reason eggs break.

## Entropy

The arrow we experience comes from thermodynamics. The **second law of thermodynamics** says that the total entropy of an isolated system never decreases. Heat flows from hot to cold, perfume spreads through a room, and broken eggs stay broken.

In the 1870s Ludwig Boltzmann explained what entropy is: a measure of how many microscopic arrangements (positions and velocities of molecules) look the same at the everyday scale. His formula, carved on his gravestone in Vienna, is

```
S = k log W
```

where W is the number of such arrangements and k is the Boltzmann constant. There are vastly more ways for an egg's molecules to be spread across the floor than to form an intact egg. So a system wandering randomly among its possible arrangements will almost certainly move from fewer ways (low entropy) to more ways (high entropy). The second law is not absolute in the way the conservation of energy is; it is overwhelmingly probable. For anything large enough to see, the odds against a spontaneous reversal are so large that it would not be expected even once in the age of the universe.

Small systems are different. For a few molecules, or a tiny bead in water watched for a fraction of a second, entropy can briefly decrease, and the **fluctuation theorems** of statistical physics, confirmed in laboratory experiments since 2002, describe exactly how often.

## The past hypothesis

Boltzmann's argument contains a puzzle. The same reasoning that predicts entropy will be higher tomorrow also "predicts" that it was higher yesterday, since the laws do not prefer a direction. Run the statistics backwards from a half-melted ice cube, and the most likely past is a puddle that happened to freeze partly, not a full ice cube.

The resolution most physicists accept is that the universe **began in a state of extraordinarily low entropy**, and has been moving toward higher entropy ever since. The philosopher David Albert named this assumption the **past hypothesis**. The arrow of time then points away from the Big Bang. Every local arrow, from cooling coffee to ageing bodies, borrows its direction from that initial condition.

What made the early universe special is subtle. The cosmic microwave background shows that 380,000 years after the beginning, matter was spread out almost perfectly evenly, at almost exactly the same temperature everywhere. For a gas in a box, that would be high entropy. But with gravity, uniformity is the *low*-entropy state: matter tends to clump into stars, galaxies and eventually black holes, and each clump releases heat and increases total entropy. Roger Penrose estimated that the initial state was special to an almost unimaginable degree, one part in 10 to the power 10^123. **Why** the universe started that way is one of the deepest open questions in cosmology. See [The beginning](/time/beginning).

## Many arrows, one direction

Physicists distinguish several arrows, which all point the same way:

- **Thermodynamic**: entropy increases.
- **Cosmological**: the universe expands.
- **Radiative**: waves spread outward from their sources, never converge on them spontaneously.
- **Psychological**: we remember the past, not the future.
- **Causal**: causes precede their effects.
- **Quantum**: measurements produce definite records that cannot be undone (the details depend on the interpretation of quantum mechanics).

Most of them can be traced to the thermodynamic one. Memory, for example, is a physical record. Writing a record, whether in a brain, on paper or on a hard drive, increases entropy overall, so records can only be made of the past. A computer also pays a minimum thermodynamic cost to erase one bit of information, kT ln 2, as Rolf Landauer showed in 1961 (measured experimentally in 2012).

The cosmological arrow seems to be independent: the expansion is not caused by entropy. Whether the arrow would reverse if the universe ever recollapsed was once debated (Stephen Hawking briefly thought so, then changed his mind). The current view is that it would not.

## Is the arrow fundamental?

Two broad views exist. In the first, which most physicists hold, the arrow is not in the laws but in the **boundary condition**: the low-entropy beginning. In the second, held by a minority, time's direction is built into the laws themselves, perhaps through some not-yet-discovered physics of quantum gravity or the measurement process. **Evidence:** the first view is well supported as an explanation of everyday irreversibility; why the initial state had low entropy is **open**.
