A clock has two parts: something that repeats regularly, and something that counts the repetitions. The history of timekeeping is a search for better repeaters. Each new one made the previous best look like a rough guess.

| Era | Repeater | Typical error |
|---|---|---|
| ~1500 BCE | Sun's shadow, dripping water | a quarter of an hour per day |
| ~1300 CE | Mechanical escapement (verge and foliot) | ~15 minutes per day |
| 1656 | Pendulum (Huygens) | ~10 seconds per day, soon better |
| 1761 | Marine chronometer (Harrison's H4) | ~5 seconds in 81 days at sea |
| 1927 | Quartz crystal | ~0.1 second per day, later much less |
| 1955 | Caesium atom (microwave) | ~1 second in 300 years, then 300 million |
| 2020s | Optical lattice and ion clocks | under 1 second in 13.8 billion years |

## Shadows and water

The oldest surviving sundials and shadow clocks are Egyptian, from the second millennium BCE. A shadow clock is honest only when the Sun shines, and its "hours" stretch and shrink with the seasons.

Water clocks worked at night and indoors. The oldest surviving one, a stone vessel with a small hole near its base, comes from the temple of Karnak and dates to the reign of Amenhotep III, around 1400 BCE. In China, water clocks (lòukè, 漏刻) divided the day into 100 *kè* and were refined over two thousand years, with stacks of tanks to keep the flow steady.

## The escapement

The key invention of mechanical timekeeping is the escapement: a device that lets a driving force (falling water, a weight, a spring) advance in fixed steps rather than all at once.

The earliest known escapement was hydro-mechanical. In 725 CE the monk Yi Xing and the engineer Liang Lingzan built a water-driven armillary sphere in China that advanced in steps. Its most famous descendant is the astronomical clock tower that Su Song completed in Kaifeng in 1092: over ten metres tall, driven by a water wheel and a chain drive, turning an armillary sphere and a celestial globe and sounding the hours with bells, drums and puppets. Su Song's book, *Xin Yixiang Fayao* (新仪象法要), describes it in enough detail that working models have been rebuilt.

In Europe, weight-driven clocks with a verge-and-foliot escapement appeared in the late 13th century, in church towers and monasteries. They were impressive but poor timekeepers, drifting by a quarter of an hour a day, and had to be reset by sundial.

## The pendulum

Galileo noticed that a pendulum's period hardly depends on the size of its swing. In 1656 Christiaan Huygens built the first pendulum clock, and timekeeping improved roughly fifty-fold almost at once. Within a century, the best astronomical regulators kept time to a second a day or better. In the 1920s the Shortt-Synchronome free-pendulum clock, with one pendulum swinging undisturbed in a vacuum tank and a second "slave" pendulum doing the work, kept time to about a second a year. It was among the first clocks steady enough to begin revealing the irregularities of the Earth's rotation, which quartz clocks then confirmed.

## Time at sea

At sea, time is position. The Earth turns 15 degrees an hour, so a navigator who knows the time at a home port and the local time from the Sun knows the longitude. An error of one minute means an error of up to 28 kilometres at the equator. After a long history of shipwrecks, the British Longitude Act of 1714 offered a large prize for a practical method. John Harrison, a carpenter by training, spent most of his life building marine timekeepers. His fourth, H4, a large watch, lost only 5.1 seconds on the 81-day voyage to Jamaica in 1761 and 1762.

## Quartz

Quartz is piezoelectric: squeeze it and a voltage appears; apply a voltage and it bends. A precisely cut crystal in an electronic circuit rings at a stable frequency. In 1927 Warren Marrison and J. W. Horton at Bell Telephone Laboratories built the first quartz clock. By the 1930s quartz clocks had shown that the Earth's day varies by about a millisecond with the seasons.

In 1969 Seiko sold the first quartz wristwatch, the Astron. Almost every watch, phone and computer now keeps time with a quartz crystal, usually one that vibrates 32,768 times a second (2^15, easy to halve in steps down to exactly one tick per second). A typical quartz watch keeps time to about 15 seconds a month.

## Atomic clocks

The first atomic clock, built at the US National Bureau of Standards in 1949, used ammonia molecules and was not yet better than quartz. In 1955 Louis Essen and Jack Parry at the UK National Physical Laboratory built the first accurate caesium clock. It led directly to the atomic definition of the second in 1967 (see [What is a second?](/time/second)).

Later improvements included the hydrogen maser (1960), very stable over hours to days, and the caesium fountain (1990s), in which laser-cooled atoms are thrown upward and fall back through a microwave field, giving a long, quiet observation time. Fountain clocks now realise the second to a few parts in 10^16.

## Optical clocks

The frequency of visible light is too fast for electronics to count. The optical frequency comb, developed around 2000 and recognised with the 2005 Nobel Prize in Physics for John Hall and Theodor Hänsch, solved that: a laser emits a "ruler" of evenly spaced frequencies that links optical frequencies to countable microwave ones.

Two designs lead. Optical lattice clocks, proposed by Hidetoshi Katori in 2001, trap thousands of strontium or ytterbium atoms in a lattice of laser light tuned so that the trap does not disturb the clock transition. Ion clocks hold a single charged atom, such as aluminium or ytterbium, in an electromagnetic trap. In 2024 and 2025 groups at JILA and NIST in the United States reported systematic uncertainties below 10^-18. At that level a clock would not gain or lose a second in the age of the universe.

Clocks this good are sensitive to gravity: raise one by a centimetre and it runs measurably faster (see [Relativity](/time/relativity)). That turns clocks into instruments for measuring the shape of the Earth's gravity field, a field called chronometric geodesy.

China has flown a cold-atom clock in space: CACES, on the Tiangong-2 space laboratory in 2016, the first laser-cooled atomic clock to operate in orbit.

## Next: a nuclear clock

Atomic clocks use transitions of electrons. The nucleus of thorium-229 has an unusually low-energy transition that can be reached with an ultraviolet laser. In 2024 groups in Vienna and at JILA excited it with lasers for the first time and measured its frequency against a strontium clock. A nuclear clock could be less sensitive to outside disturbances and may test whether the constants of nature are really constant.
