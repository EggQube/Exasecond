---
title: What is a second?
description: From a sixtieth of a sixtieth of an hour to 9,192,631,770 oscillations of a caesium atom, and the optical clocks that may replace it.
---
The second is the base unit of time in the International System of Units, and the most precisely realised unit of any kind. It has been redefined twice, and a third redefinition is being prepared. Each time the reason was the same: clocks had become better than the thing they were measuring.

## A second of the day

The units themselves are ancient. Egyptian astronomers divided night and day into twelve hours each. Babylonian mathematics counted in base 60, and Greek astronomers adopted it for angles and fractions. Medieval scholars wrote of the *pars minuta prima*, the first small part (the minute), and the *pars minuta secunda*, the second small part. That is where the word "second" comes from: the second division of the hour by sixty.

For most of history the second was simply 1/86,400 of a mean solar day. That definition made the Earth the master clock. It worked until clocks became good enough to show that the Earth is not a steady clock at all.

## The Earth is a wobbly clock

In the 1930s quartz clocks revealed that the day changes length by about a millisecond over the seasons, as winds and ocean currents trade angular momentum with the solid Earth. Astronomers also knew that tidal friction from the Moon slowly lengthens the day, by about 1.8 milliseconds per century averaged over the last few thousand years, as reconstructed from ancient eclipse records. Earthquakes, the melting of ice sheets and motions in the Earth's liquid core shift it further.

A unit that changes with the weather could not stay the foundation of physics.

## The ephemeris second (1956 to 1967)

The first fix was to use the Earth's orbit instead of its spin. In 1956 the International Committee for Weights and Measures defined the second as 1/31,556,925.9747 of the tropical year 1900. The 11th General Conference on Weights and Measures confirmed it in 1960. This "ephemeris second" was stable, but nobody could hold it in a laboratory. Realising it meant observing the Moon and planets for years.

## The atomic second (1967 to today)

Atoms offered something better: every atom of a given isotope is identical, and its internal energy levels are fixed by the laws of physics. When an electron moves between two levels, it absorbs or emits radiation at one exact frequency. Count the waves and you have a clock that is the same everywhere in the universe.

In 1955 Louis Essen and Jack Parry at the National Physical Laboratory in the United Kingdom built the first practical caesium clock. Over the next three years Essen and William Markowitz of the US Naval Observatory compared it with the ephemeris second and found that one ephemeris second equalled 9,192,631,770 cycles of the caesium transition. In 1967 the 13th General Conference adopted that number as the definition.

Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the definition reads:

> The second, symbol s, is defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the caesium frequency ΔνCs, the unperturbed ground-state hyperfine transition frequency of the caesium-133 atom, to be 9 192 631 770 when expressed in the unit Hz, which is equal to s^-1.

"Hyperfine" refers to a tiny energy difference caused by the interaction between the spin of the atom's outermost electron and the spin of its nucleus. The radiation it emits is microwave, with a wavelength of about 3.3 centimetres.

## How good is a caesium clock?

The best caesium fountain clocks, in which a cloud of laser-cooled atoms is tossed upward and falls back through a microwave cavity, now reach a systematic uncertainty of about 1 to 2 parts in 10^16. Such a clock would gain or lose about one second in 100 to 300 million years.

Because the metre, the kilogram, the ampere, the kelvin and the candela are all defined with the help of the second, the precision of time sets a floor for every other measurement.

## The optical second (expected around 2030)

Optical clocks use atoms or ions that switch between levels at the frequency of visible or ultraviolet light, tens of thousands of times higher than caesium's microwave frequency (strontium's clock transition oscillates about 429 trillion times a second). More oscillations per second means finer division of time. The best optical clocks, built on strontium or ytterbium atoms held in a lattice of laser light, or on single trapped ions of aluminium or ytterbium, have reached systematic uncertainties around 10^-18, a hundred times better than caesium. A clock at that level would drift by less than a second over the age of the universe.

The second can therefore no longer be realised as well as clocks can measure time. The Consultative Committee for Time and Frequency has published a roadmap for a new definition. Two options are under discussion: fix the frequency of one chosen optical transition, or define the second through a weighted combination of several transitions. The General Conference meeting in 2026 is to review progress; adoption of a new definition is targeted for the meeting in 2030, if the roadmap's criteria are met (among them, independent comparisons between optical clocks in different countries agreeing at the required level).

The new definition will not change the length of a second in daily life. It will make the unit sharper, the way the 1967 change did.
