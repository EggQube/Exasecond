---
title: What is time?
description: Physics measures time better than anything else, yet cannot say what it is. The open questions: the block universe, time in quantum mechanics, quantum gravity's "frozen" equations, time crystals and time travel.
---
Time is the most precisely measured quantity in science (see [Clocks](/time/clocks)). It is also one of the least understood. This chapter collects the questions physics has not answered, with the same evidence labels as the rest of this work. Where physics ends and philosophy begins, it says so.

## Absolute or relational?

Isaac Newton described "absolute, true and mathematical time" that "flows equably without relation to anything external". Gottfried Leibniz objected that time is nothing but the order of events: without change, there is no time. For two centuries Newton's view dominated, because it worked.

Relativity sided partly with Leibniz. There is no universal clock; each observer's time depends on their motion and on gravity. But relativity did not abolish time. It merged it with space into a four-dimensional **spacetime**, in which some things are absolute: the spacetime interval between events, and the order of events that could have influenced each other (causally connected events are in the same order for everyone). **Established.**

## The block universe

In 1908 Hermann Minkowski announced that "henceforth space by itself, and time by itself, are doomed to fade away into mere shadows, and only a kind of union of the two will preserve an independent reality." If there is no universal "now" (see [Relativity](/time/relativity)), it is natural to picture all of spacetime, past, present and future, as one existing four-dimensional block. On this view, called **eternalism**, the flow of time is a feature of our experience rather than of the world. Einstein wrote, in a letter after the death of his friend Michele Besso in 1955, that "the distinction between past, present and future is only a stubbornly persistent illusion."

The alternative, **presentism**, holds that only the present exists; a "growing block" view holds that the past and present exist but the future does not. **Status: philosophy.** No experiment distinguishes these views, because all of them predict the same measurements. Physics is compatible with eternalism and does not require it.

## Time in quantum mechanics

In quantum mechanics, position, momentum and energy are **observables**: quantities with operators and measurement outcomes. Time is not. It is an external parameter, read from a classical clock outside the system. In 1933 Wolfgang Pauli gave an argument that a general time operator cannot exist for systems with a lowest energy level. That is why the energy-time uncertainty relation has a different meaning from the position-momentum one: it relates how fast a system can change to how spread out its energy is, not two simultaneous measurements. **Established.**

Quantum mechanics also allows something classical physics does not: **indefinite causal order**. In the "quantum switch", two operations A and B are applied in a superposition of the orders "A then B" and "B then A". It was realised in optical experiments from 2015 onward. It does not allow sending messages into the past, but it shows that the order of events need not be definite at the quantum level. **Established in the laboratory; its meaning for spacetime is open.**

## The problem of time

General relativity treats time as part of a dynamic spacetime; quantum mechanics treats it as a fixed external parameter. A theory of **quantum gravity** has to reconcile them. When John Wheeler and Bryce DeWitt applied the rules of quantum mechanics to the whole universe in 1967, the resulting equation, the **Wheeler-DeWitt equation**, contains no time at all. The universe's quantum state does not evolve; it simply is. This "frozen formalism" is called **the problem of time**.

Proposed answers include:

- **Relational time.** Don Page and William Wootters showed in 1983 that a static universe can contain subsystems that look as if they evolve, when one subsystem is used as a clock for the rest: time emerges from correlations (entanglement) between a clock and everything else. A tabletop demonstration with entangled photons was published in 2014. **Proposed; the toy model works, its application to the universe is open.**
- **Thermal time.** Alain Connes and Carlo Rovelli proposed in 1994 that the flow of time is determined by the statistical (thermal) state of a system, not by the laws themselves. **Proposed.**
- **Time without time.** Julian Barbour argues that the universe is a collection of "nows", configurations without any time, and that the appearance of history comes from records within each configuration. **Speculative.**
- **Emergent spacetime.** In some approaches derived from string theory, spacetime geometry itself emerges from patterns of quantum entanglement (Mark Van Raamsdonk, 2010, and later work). **Proposed; developed mainly in model universes, not ours.**

## Is time continuous?

In every tested theory, time is a continuum: there is no smallest step. Some approaches to quantum gravity suggest it might not be at the Planck scale (about 10^-43 seconds). **Causal set theory** (Rafael Sorkin and others, since 1987) models spacetime as a discrete set of events with a causal order: "order plus number equals geometry".

There are indirect tests. Many discrete models would make light of different energies travel at slightly different speeds. In 2009 the Fermi telescope observed a gamma-ray burst, GRB 090510, in which high- and low-energy photons that had travelled 7 billion years arrived within a second of each other, ruling out the simplest such effects at the Planck scale. **Status: no evidence for discreteness; several models remain consistent with the data.**

## Time crystals

In 2012 Frank Wilczek asked whether matter could repeat itself in time the way a crystal repeats itself in space. In equilibrium it cannot, as was soon proven. But driven quantum systems can: a **discrete time crystal**, pushed periodically, settles into a rhythm at a multiple of the driving period, robustly, without absorbing energy. The first ones were observed in 2017, in a chain of trapped ions and in defects in diamond, and in 2021 on Google's Sycamore quantum processor. **Established.** Time crystals are a new phase of matter. They do not violate thermodynamics and are not perpetual motion machines.

## Time travel

**Forward** time travel is real and routine: an astronaut who spends six months on the International Space Station returns about five thousandths of a second younger than if they had stayed on the ground, because their speed slows their clock more than the weaker gravity speeds it up (see [Relativity](/time/relativity)).

**Backward** time travel is another matter. General relativity has solutions with **closed timelike curves**, paths through spacetime that return to their own past: Kurt Gödel's rotating universe (1949), and traversable wormholes (Michael Morris and Kip Thorne, 1988) if matter with negative energy were available in large amounts. Stephen Hawking proposed in 1992 a **chronology protection conjecture**: the laws of physics, probably through quantum effects, prevent closed timelike curves from forming. **Status: no evidence that backward time travel is possible; the question is open in principle.**

## The present moment

Physics has no special place for "now". Every moment is described the same way. Yet experience is organised around a moving present. Psychology and neuroscience study how the brain builds that experience, integrating events over roughly a few seconds and adjusting for the different speeds of sight and sound. Whether the flowing "now" is something the physical world has, or something minds add, is one of the oldest open questions there is.
