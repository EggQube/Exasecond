"Big Bang" is two things that are often confused. The **Big Bang theory** is the well-tested description of how the universe expanded and cooled from a hot, dense state, starting a tiny fraction of a second after "time zero". The **moment of the Big Bang**, time zero itself, is where that description breaks down. About the first, cosmology is confident. About the second, it has hypotheses.

This chapter uses four labels. **Established**: confirmed by many independent measurements. **Supported**: fits the evidence well and has passed real tests, but alternatives remain. **Proposed**: a serious, mathematically worked-out hypothesis without decisive evidence. **Speculative**: an idea that current or near-future data cannot test.

## What the Big Bang theory says

**Established.** The universe has been expanding for about 13.8 billion years. Early on it was hot and dense enough to be a plasma of particles and light. As it expanded it cooled, forming nuclei in the first minutes, atoms after 380,000 years, and stars and galaxies later. The main pieces of evidence are independent of each other:

- **The expansion**: galaxies recede at speeds proportional to their distance (Lemaître 1927, Hubble 1929).
- **The cosmic microwave background**: a nearly perfect thermal glow at 2.725 kelvin filling all of space (Penzias and Wilson 1965), with the exact blackbody spectrum predicted for a hot early universe (COBE, 1990).
- **The light elements**: the measured amounts of hydrogen, helium and deuterium match predictions of nuclear fusion in the first minutes.
- **Evolution**: distant (and therefore earlier) galaxies look systematically younger, and the CMB was measurably hotter in the past.

It is not an explosion *in* space, from a centre. Space itself expands, everywhere. There is no edge and no centre, as far as observations can tell.

A competing theory, the **steady state** model of Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold and Fred Hoyle (1948), proposed an eternal universe with matter continuously created. It was abandoned in the 1960s because it could not explain the cosmic microwave background or why the distant universe looks different. Hoyle himself coined the name "Big Bang", on BBC radio in 1949.

## The singularity

Run the equations of general relativity backwards and the density and temperature grow without limit at a finite time in the past: a **singularity**. Roger Penrose (1965) and Stephen Hawking (1970, with Penrose) proved that under quite general conditions, this is unavoidable in classical general relativity. Penrose received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics for this line of work.

But general relativity ignores quantum mechanics, and at densities near the Planck scale quantum effects of gravity must matter. Most physicists read the singularity not as a real event but as the point where the theory stops being trustworthy. **What actually happened there is unknown.** Every idea below is an attempt to say what replaces it.

## Inflation

**Supported.** Proposed by Alan Guth in 1981, building on work by Alexei Starobinsky, and developed by Andrei Linde, Andreas Albrecht and Paul Steinhardt, inflation says that a very brief period of exponential expansion preceded the hot Big Bang. It explains three puzzles at once:

- **Flatness**: why space is so close to geometrically flat (measured to within about 0.2 percent).
- **Horizon**: why regions of the CMB that could never have been in contact have the same temperature to one part in 100,000.
- **Seeds**: where the small density ripples that grew into galaxies came from. Inflation stretches quantum fluctuations to cosmic size.

Inflation made predictions that were later confirmed: the ripples are almost the same strength on all scales but slightly stronger on large ones (the "spectral index" measured by Planck is 0.965, just below 1, as inflation predicts), and they have the statistical properties expected of quantum fluctuations.

A key prediction is still missing: **primordial gravitational waves**, which would leave a twisting "B-mode" pattern in the polarisation of the CMB. In 2014 the BICEP2 team announced a detection, which turned out to be caused by dust in our own galaxy. The current upper limit on their strength (BICEP/Keck, 2021) has already ruled out some of the simplest inflation models. The Simons Observatory in Chile and the planned Japanese LiteBIRD satellite will search further. Inflation also leaves its own beginning unexplained.

## Eternal inflation and the multiverse

**Speculative.** In many inflation models, inflation never stops everywhere at once. It ends in some regions, producing "bubble universes" like ours, while continuing elsewhere forever. Combined with string theory, which may allow an enormous number of possible vacuum states, this leads to the idea of a multiverse with different physical constants in different bubbles. Supporters see it as a natural consequence of the theory; critics argue that a theory that predicts every outcome somewhere predicts nothing in particular. Searches for traces of collisions with other bubbles in the CMB have found nothing so far.

## The no-boundary proposal

**Proposed.** In 1983 James Hartle and Stephen Hawking proposed a quantum state for the whole universe in which, near the beginning, time behaves like a direction of space. The universe then has no initial edge, much as the surface of the Earth has no edge at the South Pole: asking what came "before" is like asking what is south of the South Pole. Alexander Vilenkin proposed a related idea in 1982: the universe "tunnelling from nothing". These ideas give the beginning a mathematical form. Whether the calculations are well defined is still debated, and they have not produced a test that distinguishes them from other ideas.

## A bounce

**Proposed.** In **loop quantum cosmology**, an application of loop quantum gravity developed since the mid-2000s by Abhay Ashtekar, Martin Bojowald and others, quantum geometry makes gravity repulsive at near-Planck densities. A contracting earlier universe would then rebound into the expansion we see: a **Big Bounce** instead of a singularity. Other bounce models exist in other frameworks. Some predict small features in the largest-scale patterns of the CMB, which have not been seen clearly.

## Cyclic universes

**Proposed.** The **ekpyrotic** and **cyclic** models (Justin Khoury, Burt Ovrut, Paul Steinhardt and Neil Turok, 2001 onward) replace inflation with a slow contraction followed by a bounce, possibly repeating. They make a clear, testable prediction: **no detectable primordial gravitational waves**. A detection of B-modes would rule them out, while continued non-detection keeps them alive.

**Speculative.** Roger Penrose's **conformal cyclic cosmology** (2010) proposes that the infinitely expanded, empty end of one universe becomes the Big Bang of the next, through a mathematical rescaling. Claims to have seen its signatures in the CMB (circles, and "Hawking points") have been disputed by other analyses.

## Did time begin?

There is no agreed answer. In some of these ideas time begins (the no-boundary proposal); in others it extends into a previous phase (bounces, cycles); in eternal inflation our Big Bang is a local event in an older, larger universe. "What happened before the Big Bang?" is therefore not a meaningless question, but it is one that physics cannot yet answer.

What would help: a detection or a tight limit on primordial gravitational waves, better measurements of the largest-scale patterns in the CMB, and ultimately a tested theory of quantum gravity.
